Collector's Guide

A true first edition is a matter of millimetres: spine cloth, endpapers, a code on the back cover. Here is how to read the fingerprints.

Tintin albums were printed in enormous numbers — but true first editions are rare, and small printing differences separate a €20 reprint from a €5,000 first state. This guide covers the essentials: the general checklist, the famous B8 and B11 codes of the moon albums, values, and condition grading.

The seven-point first edition checklist

Applies to the 1940s–1950s colour albums (Unicorn, Rackham, moon albums)

1. Red cloth spine — never green, never square paper
2. Dark blue endpapers with white character silhouettes (light-blue portrait endpapers came in 1957)
3. Short back-cover list ending at or near the album's own title
4. No ISBN — ISBNs arrived in the 1970s
5. Original printed price in Belgian francs (French edition) or Dutch guilders (Dutch edition)
6. 62 full-colour pages
7. Copyright date on the title page: 1943 (Unicorn) / 1944 (Rackham)

The moon albums: B8 and B11

The back-cover code is the collector's best friend. Casterman's early printings carried letter-number codes that identify exactly when a copy was printed.

And the rarest of all: the early B8 state printed around September 1953, while Explorers on the Moon was still being serialised in the magazine — a copy whose back cover lacks the promotional line "En préparation: On a marché sur la Lune" that Casterman added to the large 1954 print runs. (This is exactly the variant in my own collection.)

Two more dates every collector knows: the dark-blue silhouette endpapers were replaced by light-blue portrait endpapers in 1957, and the red cloth spines gave way to green square paper spines (code B35) from 1964.

How the market evolved

The moon albums' price history is a lesson in how collecting works. From 1970 to 1990, they were almost overlooked — serious collectors chased the old black-and-white albums, and first-edition moon albums traded cheaply. From 1990 to 2010, the international collector market expanded and first printings appreciated strongly. Since 2010, online auction platforms have driven explosive growth: the moon albums are now considered "iconic entry-level masterworks" — the piece many serious collectors start a Tintin collection with. Dutch first printings are harder to find in high grade than French ones, and condition has an exponential effect: a Near Mint copy typically commands 3 to 5 times the value of a Fine one.

For the older albums, the reference market is a different world. The 1930 Soviets first edition has no modern equal, but its later printings also trade: the 1969 private edition at €5,000–15,000, the 1973 reissue at €500–2,000, and modern facsimiles at €20–150. For Tintin in the Congo, the 1931 black-and-white first edition anchors the market, while the 1946 first colour edition trades at €1,000–5,000 and later colour reissues at €50–500.

And the practical advice from the collection notes: 1950s paper contains wood and browns at the edges — store albums vertically, out of direct sunlight (the red cloth spine fades), and not squeezed tightly between other books (the delicate cloth binding crushes).
