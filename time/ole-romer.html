Ole Rømer & the Speed of Light

In 1676 a Danish astronomer working in Paris proved that light is not instant — and measured its speed with a clock he could never touch: a moon of Jupiter, eclipsed every 42½ hours.

Before 1676, most scientists believed light travelled instantly — zero travel time. Ole Christensen Rømer proved otherwise, and in doing so made the first measurement of the speed of light in history: about 220,000 km per second, only 25% below the real value of 299,792 km/s. This is his story, in simple words.

Who he was

Ole Christensen Rømer was born in Aarhus, Denmark, in 1644. At 28 he went to Paris to work at the Royal Observatory under Giovanni Domenico Cassini — at the time the best-equipped astronomy workshop in the world. There, in 1676, he made his great discovery.

In 1681 he returned to Denmark as professor of astronomy in Copenhagen, in charge of the observatory in the Round Tower (Rundetårn). He was also a doer outside astronomy: he gave Copenhagen its first street lamps, standardised Danish weights and measures, brought the modern (Gregorian) calendar to Denmark in 1700, invented his own temperature scale around 1702 — and ended his career as mayor of Copenhagen and head of its police. He died in 1710, aged 66.

The moving picture: how he found it out

Io’s table time: one eclipse every 42½ h, never late. Rømer’s telescope disagreed. Distances and sizes are not to scale, and eclipses are shown far fewer than the real ~200 a year. Over half a year the lag grows to about 22 minutes — Rømer’s measured value.

Why the clock runs late

When Earth is on the far side of its orbit, light from Io has to cross the entire width of Earth’s orbit — about 300 million km of extra distance. That extra stretch is exactly what the 22 minutes pay for.

The extra 300 million kilometres
Same Sun, same Jupiter — but Earth is on the far side six months later, and light has to travel the whole width of Earth’s orbit on top of the usual trip.

How he did it, in four steps

  • 01A clock in the sky. Io, discovered by Galileo in 1610, orbits Jupiter every 42½ hours. Every lap it slips behind Jupiter and disappears: an eclipse. It ticks forever, with no winding needed.
  • 02Timing the ticks. Rømer and his Paris colleagues timed eclipse after eclipse and built tables: “next eclipse: 22:00:00”. The rhythm never changes, so the tables should always be right.
  • 03The clock ran late. It did not. The eclipses arrived late when Earth moved away from Jupiter, early when Earth moved toward it. In 1676 Rømer predicted the 9 November eclipse would come 10 minutes late — and he was right. (Cassini had seen the drift in the data, but did not believe light had a speed.)
  • 04Light needs time. The only explanation: light takes time to travel. Divide the extra distance by the extra time — 300 million km ÷ 22 minutes — and you get ≈ 220,000 km per second. Not instant. A speed. The first one ever measured.

Why it still matters

GPS. Every satellite signal arrives with a built-in correction for the time light needs to reach you — Rømer’s discovery, running inside your phone.

Light-years. The unit only exists because light has a speed. Before 1676, “distance to the stars” could not even be expressed that way.

Space missions. Signals to Moon and Mars landers take seconds to minutes. Mission planners count on exactly the delay Rømer found.
