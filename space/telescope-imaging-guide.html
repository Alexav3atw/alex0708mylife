The Complete Imaging Guide

For the owner of a Bresser MC-152/2250 and an SVBONY SV705C: from a cold telescope to a sharp, stacked picture of the Moon or a planet — every setting, click by click, with free software only.

Last verified 15 November 2026 — every number and setting was checked against official Bresser and SVBONY sources and the standard planetary-imaging workflow. The setup and shopping list live on the Telescope page.

Quick start — capture the Moon tonight

The ten-minute version (the phases below explain everything):

  1. Set the telescope outside 60–90 minutes before you start — a warm Mak shows a boiling image.
  2. Level the tripod, polar-align the EXOS-2 on Polaris, run a 2-star alignment in the ExploreStars app.
  3. Centre your target with the 26 mm eyepiece, then swap it for the camera.
  4. Screw the 1.25″ UV/IR-cut filter onto the SV705C nose, insert the camera into the focuser, connect the USB 3.0 cable (the blue port).
  5. In SharpCap: Colour Space = RAW8, Debayer Preview = On, Exposure 5–15 ms, Gain until the histogram peak sits at 60–75%.
  6. Focus on a crater or a planet edge — coarse knob first, then the 1:8 microfocuser.
  7. Output format = SER, capture 60–90 s of the Moon (2,000–3,000 frames).
  8. Stack in AutoStakkert!4 (best 20%), sharpen in RegiStax 6.

The idea: lucky imaging

You never take a photo of a planet — you film it. The atmosphere bends the light constantly (this is called seeing), so every single frame is distorted differently. The trick: record thousands of short video frames, then let free software automatically pick the sharpest moments, lay them exactly on top of each other (stacking) and sharpen the result. Every now and then the air is momentarily calm — those are the “lucky” frames, and that is where all the detail lives.

Phase 0 — install the free software

Four free programs on your Windows laptop (on Mac/Linux, FireCapture covers the same capture ground), plus GIMP for the optional final polish. The stacker and the sharpener are the same programs as in the D300 workflow:

  1. SVBONY Camera Driver — https://www.svbony.com/support-software-driver/ (also install the ASCOM platform if you plan to autoguide later).
  2. SharpCap (capture) — https://www.sharpcap.co.uk/sharpcap/downloads — the free version has everything Moon and planet capture needs.
  3. AutoStakkert!4 (stacking) — https://www.autostakkert.com/wp/download/ — the standard for aligning and stacking videos.
  4. RegiStax 6 (wavelet sharpening) — https://www.astronomie.be/registax/ — the same sharpener as the D300 workflow.
  5. GIMP (optional final polish) — https://www.gimp.org — levels, colour and export, shared with the D300 workflow.

Phase 1 — set up outside

  1. Cooldown (60–90 minutes): put the telescope outside so the mirror and the thick meniscus lens reach outside temperature. Fit a dew shield on the front.
  2. Polar alignment & GoTo: level the tripod with the north leg pointing north, roughly align the EXOS-2 on Polaris, then run a 2-star or 3-star alignment in the ExploreStars app.
  3. Centre visually: find your target (Jupiter, or a lunar crater) with the included 26 mm eyepiece and the star diagonal, and put it exactly in the middle of the field.
  4. Mount the camera (prime focus):
    • Remove the eyepiece and the 90° star diagonal.
    • Leave the included Hexafoc extension tube(s) between the tube and the focuser in place — they set the back-focus distance for camera use; without them the camera may not reach focus.
    • Screw the 1.25″ UV/IR-cut filter onto the nose piece of the SV705C.
    • Slide the camera straight into the 1.25″ holder of the focuser and connect it to a blue USB 3.0 port of your laptop.

Phase 2 — capture with SharpCap

  1. Select the camera: top left, Cameras → SVBONY SV705C.
  2. Focus: the image probably starts black or as one big blurry disc. Set Exposure temporarily high (e.g. 50 ms) and Gain to 250. Turn the coarse focus knob until the disc is as small as possible, then use the 1:8 microfocuser for fine detail — craters on the Moon, cloud belts on Jupiter.
  3. Colour settings (debayer): in Camera Controls set Colour Space to RAW8 (small files, maximum speed) and Debayer Preview to On so you see colour on screen while focusing.
  4. Shrink the field (ROI) for planets:
    • Moon: full resolution (3840x2160).
    • Planets: a planet is a tiny dot on a 4K sensor. Set Capture Area to 640x480 or 800x600 — the frame rate jumps from 45 fps to 150–250 fps and the video files stay small.
  5. Exposure & gain (the most important step):
    • Open the Histogram (toolbar button at the top).
    • Set Exposure between 5 ms and 15 ms — a short shutter “freezes” the seeing.
    • Raise Gain until the histogram peak sits between 60% and 75% (never above 80%, or bright areas blow out).
    • White Balance: click Auto, or set Red/Blue manually so the colour curves in the histogram line up.
  6. Format & duration:
    • Output Format: always SER files (*.ser) — never compressed AVI/MP4.
    • Click Start Capture with a fixed duration:
    TargetDurationWhy
    Jupiter90–120 sJupiter spins on its axis in 10 hours — longer videos smear the detail
    Saturn / Mars180 s (3 min)Slower rotation — more frames, less noise
    Moon60–90 sAbout 2,000–3,000 frames — plenty for a sharp stack

Phase 3 — stack with AutoStakkert!4

After the session you have a few multi-gigabyte .ser files. Now they become one clean, noise-free picture:

  1. Open: start AutoStakkert!4 and drag your .ser file into the window (or click 1) Open).
  2. Choose Image Stabilization: Planet (COG) for planets, Surface for the Moon (place the green anchor point with Ctrl + click on a contrast-rich crater rim).
  3. Analyse: click 2) Analyse. AutoStakkert scores every frame for sharpness and sorts them best to worst (visible in the green quality graph).
  4. Place alignment points (APs): in the right-hand image panel choose an AP Size of 48 (planets) or 64–104 (Moon), then click Place AP grid — hundreds of small measurement points appear across the surface.
  5. Stack settings: set Frame percentage to stack to 20 (the best 20% of frames; 10 in bad seeing, 30 in very steady air). Tick Sharpened only for a quick preview — the real sharpening happens in the next phase on the unsharpened file. Click 3) Stack — AutoStakkert writes a new .tif into a subfolder (e.g. AS_P20).

Phase 4 — sharpen in RegiStax 6

The .tif from AutoStakkert still looks soft and hazy. That is normal: all the fine detail is hidden in the data and comes out through wavelet sharpening — the same tool and the same settings approach as in the D300 workflow.

  1. Open: launch RegiStax 6 → “Select” (top-left) → open your AS_..._stack.tif from AutoStakkert (not the _conv variant).
  2. Colour balance: on the right, RGB Balance → Auto Balance — any colour cast disappears. For planets with a red/blue edge from the atmosphere: tab RGB Align → Estimate → Do All.
  3. Sharpen (wavelets): go to the Wavelet tab — six layers, finest (1) to coarsest (6).
    • Planets: raise layers 2–3 first (the disc’s detail lives there), layer 1 just a touch.
    • Moon: raise layers 1–3 gently — layers 1–2 carry the crater detail.
    • Per layer: set Denoise to about 0.10–0.30 (more if the image is noisy).
  4. How much is too much? bright rings around craters or around the planet edge (“onion rings”) and a plastic look mean: too far. Back the layers off, raise denoise.
  5. Click “Do All” (applies the wavelets to the full image) → then “Save Image” (top bar) → save as TIFF or PNG.
RegiStax says “invalid TIFF header”?

Known quirk with some TIFF variants. Open the file in GIMP → File → Export As… → PNG → open that PNG in RegiStax.

Optional final polish with GIMP

  1. Colors → Levels: move the white/black triangles inward until the histogram touches the data — the background turns true black.
  2. Colors → Color Balance: Saturn likes a warm yellow push in the highlights, Jupiter a gentle warm cream — subtle is enough (±10).
  3. File → Export As… → PNG or JPEG (quality 90) to share.

Troubleshooting

SymptomCauseFix
Image “boils” at high magnificationTube not cooled; or bad seeingWait for thermal equilibrium (60–90 min); wait for the planet to rise higher
Camera cannot reach focusWrong back-focus distanceKeep the Hexafoc extension tube(s) in place for prime focus
Soft, washed-out detail on the MoonInfrared light (AR window passes IR)Screw the 1.25″ UV/IR-cut filter onto the camera nose
Histogram over 80%Gain too highLower Gain (and/or Exposure) until the peak sits at 60–75%
Planet drifts out of frame during captureRough polar alignmentRe-align; keep the ROI generous (640x480) — AutoStakkert centres the rest
GoTo connection dropsWeak voltage or Wi-Fi congestionStable 12 V supply (2–5 A); or connect the mount by mini-USB
Sharp but grainy after waveletsToo much sharpening, too few framesBack off the layers; raise Denoise per layer (0.10–0.30)

Glossary

TermMeaning
Lucky imagingFilming thousands of short frames and stacking only the sharpest
SeeingHow calm the atmosphere is — decides planetary sharpness
Prime focusThe camera sits directly in the focuser without eyepiece or lens
Back focusThe distance behind the focuser where the image comes to focus
ROIRegion Of Interest: reading out only part of the sensor → much higher frame rate
SERThe lossless video format for planetary imaging (never compressed AVI/MP4)
RAW8Unprocessed 8-bit sensor data — small files, full speed
DebayerTurning raw colour-filter-sensor data into a colour image
StackingLaying many frames exactly on top of each other and averaging → more detail, less noise
WaveletsSharpening that works per detail level (RegiStax 6)
AP (alignment point)Reference point AutoStakkert uses to align the frames
Nyquist samplingRule: ideal f-ratio ≈ 5 × pixel size in µm — f/14.5 for the 2.9 µm IMX585
UV/IR-cutFilter blocking ultraviolet and infrared light — mandatory with the SV705C
