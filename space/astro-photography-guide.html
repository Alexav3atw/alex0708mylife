The Complete D300 Guide

For the absolute beginner with a Nikon D300 and an AF-S VR 70–300 mm f/4.5–5.6G: the basics, every single camera setting, the shooting plans for Moon and planets, and the post-processing that makes them sharp.

Last verified 15 November 2026 — every setting, number and software version was checked against the official Nikon manual and current sources. The printed companion is the field cheat sheet.

Quick start — shoot the Moon tonight

The ten-minute version (the full explanations follow in the chapters below):

  1. Tripod up, camera mounted, VR on the lens set to OFF (crucial on a tripod!).
  2. MENU → Custom Setting Menu → d9 “Exposure delay mode” → On — the mirror goes up, the camera waits about a second, then fires: no vibration.
  3. MENU → Shooting Menu: Image quality = RAW (NEF), NEF bit depth = 12-bit, compression = Lossless, ISO = 200, Active D-Lighting = Off.
  4. Release mode dial on S (single frame). Camera in M (manual), lens switch on M (manual focus).
  5. Live View on (LV dial position) → choose tripod mode (MENU → Shooting Menu → Live view → Live view mode → Tripod) → zoom in with the magnify button (⊕) → focus on the Moon’s edge until the craters snap into place → Live View can stay on (in tripod mode the mirror is already up).
  6. Exposure: aperture f/8, ISO 200, shutter speed from this table:
Moon phase (November 2026)Shutter speed at f/8, ISO 200
Full moon (24 Nov, high in the sky)1/400 s
Between quarter and full (gibbous)1/200 s
Waxing Moon this week — heading to first quarter (17 Nov)1/100 s (try 1/50–1/200 and check the blinkies)
Wide crescent1/50 s
Thin crescent1/25 s
Moon low above the horizon+1 to +2 stops longer
  1. Take 15–25 shots in a row. One is often already nice, but stacking (chapter “Post-processing”) makes it truly sharp.

What to expect — the honest story

Before you invest nights in this: here is what your gear can do, and what it cannot.

The maths of your camera

QuantityValueMeaning
D300 sensorDX format, 23.6 × 15.8 mm, 4288 × 2848 px (12.3 MP)APS-C
Pixel size5.5 µmthe size of one light-sensitive cell
Crop factor1.5×300 mm behaves like 450 mm on full frame
Pixel scale at 300 mm3.78 arcseconds per pixelyour true “magnification”

The pixel scale is the measure that matters: how much sky fits on one pixel. The Moon has an apparent diameter of about 1860 arcseconds (0.5°). At 3.78″/px that makes the Moon about 490 pixels across — roughly one sixth of the frame height (2848 px). A beautiful, detailed Moon ball — not a frame-filling disc.

Manage your expectations

These will not be Hubble pictures. Jupiter will be a small disc of about 12 pixels on which — on a good night — you can see a band or two. The fun is squeezing the maximum out of 300 mm with smart technique — and 90% of that maximum comes from stacking, not from better hardware.

Why the full Moon is the worst Moon

At full Moon the Sun is straight behind us: craters are lit from above and cast no shadows. Result: a bright, flat, boring disc. Along the terminator (the day/night line) the Sun sits low over the lunar surface and every crater casts long shadows — that is where the detail pops. So shoot the Moon preferably between crescent and quarter phase, like this week.

Why stacking is the secret

The atmosphere is never still: layers of air at different temperatures bend the light (you see it as “twinkling” stars). A single exposure therefore captures a slightly distorted image. The trick: take 20 to 100 identical shots in quick succession — every now and then the air is momentarily calm (the “lucky” frames). Software picks the sharpest frames, lays them perfectly on top of each other and averages them: noise and atmospheric distortion average out, real detail gets reinforced.

  • Moon: a single well-focused exposure is already nice; stacking 15–50 frames makes it measurably sharper.
  • Planets: without stacking you see nothing. This is called lucky imaging.
  • The free software that does this: PIPP → AutoStakkert!4 → RegiStax 6 (chapter “Post-processing”).

Seeing and transparency

  • Seeing = how calm the air is. Bad seeing: stars twinkle violently, the Moon’s edge “boils”. In bad seeing Jupiter becomes soup no matter how much you stack. Best moments: shortly after the planet has risen high, over cold ground (grass) instead of rooftops (rising warm air disturbs the image).
  • Transparency = how clear the air is (moisture, dust, thin haze). Bad transparency makes everything fainter and greyer.
  • Never shoot through an open window and never above a heated roof — it ruins everything.

NEF (RAW) — why you asked for it

A NEF file contains the raw measurements of the sensor, not the finished picture of a JPEG. You can adjust exposure, white balance and sharpening afterwards without quality loss. JPEG throws away about 90% of the data when saving. For the Moon: NEF. For planet bursts: JPEG is allowed (explained in the planets chapter), because there speed and frame count beat file format.

Exposure mode: camera on M (manual). The meter can sit on spot or matrix — you only use it as a rough check; the tables in the Moon and planets chapters are your real guide. Lens switch on M (manual focus) once focused.

Focusing: Live View tripod mode (the most important trick)

The D300 has two Live View modes. For astro you use the tripod mode:

  1. Dial to LV → Live View starts. Choose the mode via MENU → Shooting Menu → Live view → Live view mode → Tripod (the other mode, Hand-held, flips the mirror while focusing — not what you want).
  2. Aim at the Moon (or planet). In tripod mode you focus with the AF-ON button (contrast autofocus; half-pressing the shutter does not focus in this mode). You move the AF point with the multi selector, and it blinks green when sharp. Or turn the focus ring manually.
  3. Press the magnify button (⊕) to zoom in and fine-tune. Turn until the Moon’s edge/craters (or the planet dot) are as small and sharp as possible.
  4. Set the lens to M — focus is now locked.
  5. Important: in tripod mode the mirror is already up; the exposure happens without mirror slap. So you can simply stay in Live View and shoot. (If you want planet bursts with CH, exit Live View and shoot through the viewfinder — the focus stays, because the lens is on M.)
💡 Focus tip for planets

A planet is only ±12 px — focusing on it is hard. First focus on the Moon (big and bright) if it is out, or on a bright star: it must become the smallest possible dot. Then swing to the planet without touching the focus ring.

Shooting the Moon — step by step

Preparation (10 minutes at home)

  1. Battery full, CF card empty and formatted.
  2. Settings per the chapter above (or the cheat sheet).
  3. Pick your moment: this week (waxing Moon, terminator!) or 13–21 December. The Moon stands higher in winter, so quality is better than in summer.

Exposure — the “Looney 11” rule

The classic rule of thumb: at f/11, the shutter speed equals 1/ISO. Because you work at f/8 (one stop more light), the shutter speed doubles:

Moon phaseShutter speed at f/8, ISO 200Memory aid
Full Moon, high in the sky1/400 sLooney 11: f/11 → 1/200; at f/8 → 1/400
Between quarter and full (gibbous, >50% lit)1/200 s+1 stop
First/last quarter1/100 s+2 stops
Wide crescent1/50 s+3 stops
Thin crescent1/25 s+4 stops
Moon low above the horizon+1 to +2 stops longerthe atmosphere swallows light
This week (15–17 Nov)

The Moon sits between wide crescent and first quarter: start at 1/100 s and adjust with the blinkies.

Checking — two tools in your camera:

  • Histogram (RGB): the peak should sit right of centre, without sticking against the right edge.
  • Blinkies: set playback to “Highlights” (blinking overexposed zones). The Moon may barely blink — if a large area blinks, shorten the exposure.
Do not trust the exposure meter blindly

It meters on a pitch-black sky and turns the Moon into a white blob. Spot metering on the Moon works as a rough start, but manual + the table above + blinkies is more reliable.

At the sky — the script

  1. Tripod up, camera level, VR OFF, lens at 300 mm.
  2. Aim at the Moon, focus via Live View tripod mode: zoom in, turn until the terminator (the day/night line, not the outer edge!) is razor sharp. Lens on M.
  3. Set M exposure: f/8, ISO 200, shutter speed from the table.
  4. Release dial S, d9 On (or 2 s self-timer). One press per shot.
  5. Take 15–25 shots — give the stacking software something to choose from. No refocusing needed between shots.
  6. After each series: zoom in on the screen and check sharpness. Blurry? Adjust focus, new series.
  7. Feel free to vary: 200 mm and 135 mm also give usable Moon shots with more “context”.

Shooting the planets — step by step

The principle: lucky imaging

A planet is tiny (±12 px) and the atmosphere distorts it constantly. So you take dozens to a hundred shots in a short time and the software stacks the sharpest ones. Key difference from the Moon: a planet may be exposed dark (half as bright as “correct”). You see noise on a single frame, but stacking averages noise away and recovers the brightness — overexposure, by contrast, is gone forever.

Start with these values, take a test frame and check the blinkies: the planet must NOT blink (overexposed). In doubt, pick the shorter shutter speed.

JPEG or NEF? The buffer story

The D300 shoots 6 fps, but the buffer (temporary memory) fills up fast:

Format± Frames before the buffer is fullConsequence
NEF 14-bit±13–17burst stops after ±2.5 s
NEF 12-bit±16–20burst stops after ±3 s
JPEG Fine L±35–40±6 s of continuous shooting — the best choice for planets

Therefore: shoot planets as JPEG Fine L. The small quality loss does not weigh up against twice as many frames (and lucky imaging lives on frames). If you prefer NEF, choose 12-bit and accept shorter bursts (or CL at 3–4 fps to spread the buffer). The Moon (single frames) you simply shoot as NEF.

The Saturn script (and later Jupiter)

  1. Wait until the planet is high (±30–40°) — see Stellarium.
  2. Tripod, VR OFF, 300 mm. Focus via Live View on the Moon or a bright star (see the D300 chapter); lens on M.
  3. Settings: M, f/8, ISO and shutter speed from the table, JPEG Fine L, ADL off.
  4. Release dial CH. Aim at the planet — the dot sits somewhere in the middle of the frame.
  5. Press and hold: 30–100 frames. Let the buffer drain in between (green lamp off) and repeat for 2–3 series.
  6. Between series check: is the planet still in frame (it slowly drifts away due to Earth’s rotation — ±4 pixels per second at 300 mm), is it not overexposed, is the focus still sharp?
  7. Jupiter: keep series shorter (±1–2 min per series) because of its fast rotation. The 4 Galilean moons show on every good frame as dots in a row.

Why it sometimes “fails” (and that is OK)

  • Bad seeing: the image “boils” — stacking cannot beat it. Wait a quarter of an hour or pick another night.
  • Planet = shapeless dot after stacking: normal at 300 mm when seeing is mediocre; a calm night + 3× drizzle makes the difference.
  • Saturn looks like a bean: correct! At ±11 px you see the ring as an oval, not a separation. That is already a serious achievement with a 300 mm lens.

Moon — workflow A: one nice shot (10 minutes)

The easiest route to a nice Moon photo:

  1. Open NX Studio → browse to your folder → double-click the sharpest NEF.
  2. In the right panel:
    • Exposure (Exposure Compensation): adjust until the Moon is bright but nowhere blows out to white.
    • White balance: Direct sunlight or manual ±5200 K.
    • Picture Control → Sharpness/Clarity: raise carefully (3–5) — this is where the craters “pop”.
    • Contrast: +10 to +20 gives punch.
    • Noise reduction: Low (at ISO 200 barely needed).
  3. File → Convert Files → choose JPEG (high quality) → save in fotos\maan\bewerkt\.

Moon — workflow B: stacking (sharper, 30 minutes)

Step 1 — PIPP: open PIPP → add your 15–25 Moon shots (NEF works directly) → tab “Solar/Lunar Full Disc” → Output: AVI or TIFF series → “Start Processing”. PIPP centres the Moon in every frame and removes the worst ones.

Step 2 — AutoStakkert!4: open PIPP’s output → choose Surface mode → let AS!4 place alignment points automatically (AP size 48–96 px) → “Analyse” → Stack: best 50% of the frames → output 16-bit TIFF.

Step 3 — RegiStax 6: open the stacked TIFF (not the _conv version, the unsharpened one) → tab Wavelet → nudge the lower layers carefully (layers 1–3, the fine details): each slider a few steps, watch the preview. Use some Denoise per layer. Too much = “plastic” Moon. → Do All → save as TIFF/JPEG in fotos\maan\gestackt\.

Step 4 (optional): NX Studio or GIMP for exposure, contrast and one last sharpening tick → fotos\maan\bewerkt\.

Planets — the full workflow (the most important chapter)

Step 1 — Selecting (FastStone or NX Studio): browse the burst JPEGs and delete obviously shaken frames. (PIPP also does a quality sort later, so this does not need to be perfect.)

Step 2 — PIPP: add all frames → tab “Planetary” → options: centre the planet, crop window ±300–500 px around the planet, quality sort on (keep the best 80–90% — the real selection is done by AS!4) → Output AVI or TIFF → “Start Processing”.

Step 3 — AutoStakkert!4: open the PIPP output → mode Planet (Surface) → place the alignment point (AP) on the planet (AP larger than the planet itself) → “Analyse” → Stack:

  • Jupiter: best 10–20%
  • Saturn: best 30–50%
  • Drizzle: 1.5× or 3× — enlarges the tiny planet in software before stacking (at 300 mm, 3× is recommended; ±12 px becomes ±36 px)

→ output 16-bit TIFF.

Step 4 — RegiStax 6: open the stacked TIFF → tab Wavelet → the planet lives in the fine layers: nudge layers 1–3 up carefully, with Denoise per layer, until the disc turns crisp (too far = ring-shaped artefacts, “onion rings”) → Do All → save.

Step 5 — Polish (NX Studio or GIMP): colours (Saturn: yellow-beige; Jupiter: cream with brownish bands), a light levels correction, optionally a final gentle sharpen → fotos\planeten\bewerkt\.

💡 What to expect

A stacked Saturn from 100 frames in good seeing is ±30–40 px after 3× drizzle — a recognisable oval with a ring. Jupiter sometimes shows 1–2 cloud bands and almost always its moons as dots (those sit on the single frames too — keep one!).

The golden rules of post-processing

  1. Stack before sharpening; sharpen last. Wavelets are the last big step; everything after that is cosmetic.
  2. Less is more: an over-sharpened Moon looks like plastic; an over-processed planet like a cartoon.
  3. Always keep the NEFs/JPEGs in RAW\ — in a year, with more experience, you will re-stack them and get more out of them.
  4. Something does not work? The troubleshooting chapter.

  5. Milky Way: with a wide-angle lens (e.g. the 18–55 mm kit lens at f/3.5), ISO 1600, 20–25 s, from a dark location (the Ardennes!).
  6. If it starts to itch for more: a second-hand 150–600 mm (Tamron/Sigma) or a small telescope with a T2 adapter on the D300 — planets become genuinely interesting then. (A teleconverter on the 70–300 is not officially supported by Nikon — not a good route.)
