NAT — Network Address Translation

The trick that lets a whole house share one public IP address — and the most misunderstood "security" feature in networking.

IPv4 has about 4.3 billion addresses — and the last free blocks were handed out in February 2011. Yet billions of devices still get online today. The reason is NAT: a translator built into your router that lets hundreds of private addresses share one public one.

How NAT works — step by step

Say your PC at 192.168.1.10 opens a connection to a website at 198.51.100.20:443. The packet leaves with the source address 192.168.1.10 — which is a private address, meaningless on the public internet. The router steps in:

One connection, rewritten twice
The router swaps the source address on the way out and swaps it back on the way in — remembering who asked.
  1. Outbound: the router rewrites the source to 203.0.113.5:54321 (its own public address, plus a free port), recalculates the checksums, and writes the mapping into its NAT table.
  2. The reply comes back to 203.0.113.5:54321.
  3. Inbound: the router looks up port 54321 in the table, finds your PC, rewrites the destination back to 192.168.1.10:54321 — and delivers.

The table entry that makes it work:

ProtocolInside globalInside localOutside localOutside global
TCP203.0.113.5:54321192.168.1.10:54321198.51.100.20:443198.51.100.20:443

Translation: inside local = the PC as it calls itself; inside global = the PC as the internet sees it. If port 54321 were already taken, the router would simply pick another free one. (The addresses 203.0.113.0/24 and 198.51.100.0/24 are documentation ranges — RFC 5737 — which is why every textbook uses them.)

Port forwarding — the exception to the rule

NAT is great outbound, but it blocks the other direction: nobody on the internet can reach your PC, because it has no public address. Port forwarding is the controlled exception — you pin one public port to one internal machine:

External 203.0.113.5:8080 → internal 192.168.1.20:80 (a web server on your LAN). On a Cisco router:

ip nat inside source static tcp 192.168.1.20 80 203.0.113.5 8080
The technician's port-forwarding checklist

1. Give the target host a static IP or DHCP reservation first (its address must never change).  2. Forward the right internal IP, port and protocol.  3. Allow it in the host's own firewall.  4. Test from outside the network — and remember that CGNAT (below) breaks everything anyway.

UPnP — the hole puncher

UPnP lets applications ask the router to open ports automatically. Convenient — and unauthenticated. Exposed UPnP stacks have been exploited repeatedly (Rapid7's 2013 scan found ~6,900 vulnerable products from 1,500 vendors; the 2020 CallStranger bugs enabled DDoS and data theft). The technician's advice: disable UPnP unless you need it.

CGNAT — when your ISP NATs too

Some ISPs run their own NAT — carrier-grade NAT — so many subscribers share one public address (the reserved range 100.64.0.0/10, RFC 6598, exists for exactly this). You then sit behind double NAT: your router's NAT plus the ISP's. Consequence: inbound connections fail, so port forwarding stops working — bad news for gamers, P2P and self-hosted servers. Detect it: if your router's WAN address is in 100.64.0.0/10 — or differs from what an external "what is my IP" site reports — you're behind CGNAT. The fixes: ask the ISP for a public IP, use a VPN/relay, or go IPv6.

NAT and IPv6 — and the myth

IPv6's 128-bit space has enough addresses for every grain of sand — so IPv6 doesn't need NAT for address saving. And now the myth, in RFC 4864's own words: "NAT was not developed as a security mechanism" and "it is not a security mechanism." NAT happens to block unsolicited inbound traffic — but only as a side effect of state tracking, not by design, and it blocks everything while providing no inspection. A real stateful firewall (which your router also runs) is the security layer. NAT translates; the firewall filters. They are not the same thing.
